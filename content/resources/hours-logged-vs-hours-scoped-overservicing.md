**Short answer:** Compare hours logged with hours scoped, client by client, every week. If a retainer is consuming meaningfully more hours than it was priced for, its delivery margin has already dropped, even though the invoice and your revenue dashboard have not moved.

Overservicing is easy to miss because nothing breaks. Parakeeto defines it as "providing more work, time, or value to a client than what was originally scoped, agreed upon, or compensated for", and notes that the core problem is extra work done without tracking it, charging for it, or revisiting the scope. Its practical advice is plain: log all hours on projects, including overages, and assess scope creep and overages weekly. Source: [parakeeto.com/overservicing](https://www.parakeeto.com/overservicing/).

## Why the invoice hides it

A retainer invoice is fixed. The cost of delivering it is not. Revenue reports compare this month's billing with last month's billing, so a client who quietly takes 50% more hours looks identical to one who stays inside scope. The difference only shows up when you put two numbers side by side: hours scoped when the retainer was priced, and hours the team actually logged.

## A worked example (illustrative numbers)

Suppose a retainer bills $6,000 a month, scoped at 40 hours, and loaded delivery cost is $55 an hour.

As scoped, delivery cost is 40 x $55 = $2,200, so delivery margin is ($6,000 - $2,200) / $6,000, about 63.3%. The effective rate is $6,000 / 40 = $150 per hour.

If the team logs 62 hours, delivery cost becomes 62 x $55 = $3,410. Delivery margin falls to ($6,000 - $3,410) / $6,000, about 43.2%. The effective rate falls to $6,000 / 62, about $96.77 per hour.

The invoice did not change. Margin dropped about 20 points and the effective rate dropped by more than a third. These are made-up numbers for illustration, not client data.

## A weekly check that takes ten minutes

1. List your five largest clients.
2. For each, write down hours scoped for the month and hours logged so far.
3. Compute the pace: hours logged divided by the share of the month that has passed. A client at 70% of scoped hours with only half the month gone is heading for an overrun.
4. Compute the delivery margin the current pace implies, using the same method as the example above.
5. Mark any client heading below the delivery margin you need. That client is your first conversation: re-scope, reprice, or change how the work is delivered.

Keep the cost figure honest. Use loaded delivery cost per hour, not salary alone, and say in your notes which assumption you used.

## What this does not tell you

Hours are a proxy. Some overservicing is a deliberate investment in a relationship, and some work is rightly absorbed when a client is growing. The point is not to bill every minute; it is to make the choice on purpose instead of discovering it later. This is general operating guidance, not accounting or financial advice. Your accountant or fractional CFO can tell you which benchmarks fit your business.
