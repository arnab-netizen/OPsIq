Revenue is up over last year. On paper, that should feel good. But the number that actually lands in profit hasn't moved the same way — maybe it's flat, maybe it's smaller than it was on less revenue. Nothing looks obviously broken. There's just more coming in and less left over.

That gap has a small number of usual causes, and you can usually tell which one you have from numbers you already have on hand.

## Revenue growth and profit growth are not the same number

Revenue is what came in. Profit is what's left after everything it cost to bring that revenue in. Growing the first number says nothing about what happened to the second — a business can grow revenue while its profit stays flat, shrinks, or even goes negative, depending on what changed underneath.

The useful question isn't "did revenue grow?" It's "did the *cost of each dollar of revenue* stay the same while it grew?"

## Three usual causes

**1. Pricing hasn't kept up with costs.** Your costs — materials, labor, subscriptions, rent — moved since you last set prices. If prices stayed still while input costs rose, every sale now carries a thinner margin than it used to, even at the same volume.

**2. A shift toward lower-margin work.** Not all revenue is the same shape. A customer segment, product line, or type of job that pays less per hour or per unit can grow as a *share* of total revenue even while overall revenue climbs — pulling the blended margin down without any single price changing.

**3. Overhead grew ahead of revenue.** Fixed costs — a new hire, a bigger space, added software — often get added in anticipation of growth, not in response to it already having arrived. If overhead grew faster than revenue did, more of each new dollar is going toward carrying the bigger cost base before any of it reaches profit.

These aren't mutually exclusive — it's common for two to be happening at once. But usually one of them is doing most of the work, and you can find out which by looking at gross margin, not just the top and bottom lines.

## A worked example (illustrative)

A single-location service business compares this year to last year.

Table: Worked example — year-over-year comparison (illustrative)
| Line item | Last year | This year |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | $240,000 | $300,000 |
| Direct costs (materials + direct labor) | $96,000 | $135,000 |
| Gross margin | $144,000 (60%) | $165,000 (55%) |
| Fixed overhead (rent, salaried staff, software) | $110,000 | $140,000 |
| → **Operating profit** | **$34,000** | **$25,000** |

Revenue grew 25%. Operating profit fell by about a quarter. Two things happened at once: gross margin slipped from 60% to 55% — five points of every revenue dollar now going to direct cost that didn't before — and fixed overhead grew by $30,000, faster in dollar terms than gross margin grew ($21,000). Either one alone would have dented profit; together, they turned a 25% revenue increase into a profit decrease.

The five-point margin slip points at cause #1 or #2 — a pricing gap, a mix shift toward costlier-to-deliver work, or both. The overhead jump points at cause #3 separately. Without breaking the numbers apart like this, both would be invisible behind a single "profit is down" observation.

*This example is deliberately simplified: it assumes two clean, comparable annual periods with no one-off items (asset sales, write-offs, owner compensation changes) folded into either "direct costs" or "overhead," and it isolates gross margin and overhead as the only two moving parts. A real year usually has more going on — this isolates the two most common drivers, on purpose.*

## Your self-check

- [ ] I know my gross margin percentage for this year and last year, not just total revenue and total profit.
- [ ] If gross margin moved, I can say whether it was a pricing gap, a cost increase, a mix shift toward different work, or some of each.
- [ ] I know how much my fixed overhead grew in dollar terms over the same period, separate from direct costs.
- [ ] I've compared overhead growth to gross-margin-dollar growth, not just to revenue growth.
- [ ] If there's a gap, I can name the specific line item causing most of it, rather than a general sense that "costs are just higher now."

If you get through that and find a real gap, the useful next step is the same as always: name the specific cause, decide what changes and by when, and check back to see whether it actually moved the number.
