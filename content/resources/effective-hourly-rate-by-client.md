Revenue per client is the number most agency owners see first. It is also the number that hides the most. Two clients who pay the same monthly retainer can leave you with very different amounts once you count the hours it took to serve them.

## The method in three lines

1. Effective hourly rate = monthly retainer / hours logged that month.
2. Cost per hour = your loaded delivery cost per hour (salary, benefits and the other costs of the people doing the work, divided by their working hours).
3. Delivery margin = (retainer - hours logged x cost per hour) / retainer.

Rank every retainer client by effective hourly rate or by delivery margin, lowest first. The client at the bottom of that list is where to look first.

## Illustrative worked example

These numbers are made up for illustration and are not client data. Three clients each pay $5,000 a month. Your loaded delivery cost is $55 an hour.

- Client A: 40 hours logged. Cost $2,200, $2,800 left, 56.0% margin, effective rate $125 an hour.
- Client B: 55 hours logged. Cost $3,025, $1,975 left, 39.5% margin, effective rate about $91 an hour.
- Client C: 80 hours logged. Cost $4,400, $600 left, 12.0% margin, effective rate $62.50 an hour.

All three invoices are identical. Looking only at revenue, the three accounts look equally healthy. Together they bring in $15,000 and cost $9,625 to deliver, a blended margin of about 35.8%. That blended figure hides the fact that one client earns only $7.50 an hour above what the hours cost you.

## What to do with the ranking

Pick one client and one action. For Client C, the options are to reduce the hours (agree scope with the client, or fix the work that is running over), to reprice, or to do both over a quarter. In this illustration, holding a 40% margin at 80 logged hours would need a retainer of about $7,333 a month. That is a large jump, which is why checking scope first is usually the first conversation. The companion guide on [hours logged vs hours scoped](/resources/hours-logged-vs-hours-scoped-overservicing) shows how to check that.

This is arithmetic on your own time records, not a benchmark. It tells you where the margin is going, not whether the work was worth doing. A strategic client can be worth keeping at a thin margin if you decide that on purpose. If several of your numbers look off at once, start with [which agency number to fix first](/resources/which-agency-number-to-fix-first).

## Limits

The method needs honest time tracking. If hours are logged late or unevenly, the effective rate will mislead. It also uses cost per hour, so it ignores overhead, sales effort and any one-off costs. Treat the result as a way to decide what to look at first, not as a finished profitability analysis. This is not financial advice.
