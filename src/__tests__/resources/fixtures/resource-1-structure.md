Your profit and loss statement says you made money last month. Your bank balance says otherwise. **Both can be true at the same time**, and knowing *which* problem you have decides what you should do next.

## Two different problems that look the same

Profit measures whether your work earns more than it costs. Cash measures whether money is in the account when a bill is due. They move on different clocks:

- Customers pay invoices weeks after the work is done.
- Suppliers, payroll, rent, and tax are due on fixed dates.
- Inventory and equipment consume cash before they earn anything back.

## A worked example

Table: Monthly profit
| Line | Amount | Share of revenue |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Revenue | $28,125 | 100% |
| Costs | $23,625 | 84% |
| Profit | $4,500 | 16% |

*Figures are illustrative. Your own margin and payment timing will differ.*

Table: Cash position for the next 30 days
| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Cash in the bank | $2,000 |
| Near-term obligations due | $7,000 |
| Current cash gap | $5,000 |

### What the example shows

The business is profitable at a 16% margin, yet it cannot cover $7,000 of obligations from $2,000 in the bank. That is a **cash-timing problem**, not a profit problem.

## A quick self-check

- [ ] Do you know how many days, on average, customers take to pay you?
- [ ] Do you know which bills are due in the next 30 days?
- [ ] Is your profit margin stable or rising over the last three months?
- [x] Have you compared this month's profit with this month's change in cash?

## What to do next

1. List every obligation due in the next 30 days.
2. List every invoice you expect to collect in that window.
3. Compare the two before you decide to cut costs or chase new sales.

> A profitable business that runs out of cash is still out of cash.

:::note
If the gap only appears in certain months, it is usually a timing pattern you can plan for.
:::
